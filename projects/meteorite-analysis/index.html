● PROJECT OVERVIEW

This project revisited NASA's meteorite landings dataset with a specific question in mind: where in the United States are meteorites most likely to be found? The dataset's large scale made SQL the right first step — I used it for initial cleaning and transformation before bringing the data into Tableau for visualization.

SQL cleaning involved removing null values from key columns (coordinates, mass, year), filtering to U.S. landings only, and narrowing the time window to the last century (1912–2012). This reduced the dataset to a focused, analyzable scope while preserving geographic diversity.

The resulting Tableau dashboard features an interactive map where marker size represents mass, KPI cards summarizing total meteorites and average mass, and filters to toggle between "found" and "fell" classifications and to explore specific years.

● SQL SAMPLE

SELECT

name, fall, year,

mass, reclat, reclong

FROM meteorite_landings

WHERE year BETWEEN 1912 AND 2012

AND reclong BETWEEN -125 AND -65

AND reclat BETWEEN 24 AND 50

AND mass IS NOT NULL

ORDER BY mass DESC;

● KEY FINDINGS

1

Central U.S. — particularly Texas and surrounding states — emerged as the densest hotspot for recorded meteorite landings.

2

Heavier meteorites were significantly more likely to be recorded as 'falling' rather than 'found', suggesting larger impacts leave more recoverable remnants.

3

Filtering to U.S. landings from 1912–2012 reduced the dataset to a manageable and meaningful scope for geographic analysis.

4

The central U.S. concentration points to both geological conditions and population density as factors in meteorite recovery rates.
