THE ANALYTICAL CHALLENGE

NASA's raw meteorite dataset spans over 600 years and thousands of records, but without targeted scoping and cleaning it produces misleading patterns. The question this project answers: when we focus on a reliable, modern century of data, what geographic and temporal patterns emerge in global meteorite landings?

● HOW IT WORKS

I started with NASA's full meteorite landings dataset, which contains records dating back to 1399. Rather than analyzing the entire dataset, I made a deliberate analytical decision to scope the project to 1900-2000, a century where detection technology, record-keeping standards, and geographic coverage are reliable enough to support meaningful pattern analysis.

SQL was used for all data preparation: filtering by year range, removing records with null mass or coordinates, and aggregating totals by year for the temporal analysis. The cleaned dataset was then brought into Tableau where I built four coordinated visuals: a geographic dot map, a line chart of landings per year, a treemap of total mass per year, and supporting filters.

The combination of map, time series, and treemap lets viewers explore the data spatially, temporally, and by magnitude simultaneously, each view reveals a different dimension of the same story.

SELECT name, year, mass, reclat, reclong, fall

FROM meteorite_landings

WHERE year BETWEEN 1900 AND 2000

AND mass IS NOT NULL

AND reclat IS NOT NULL

ORDER BY mass DESC;

● ANALYTICAL FINDINGS

1

1920 Was the Heaviest Year on Record

1920 recorded the highest total mass of fallen meteorites at 60,255,072g, driven by a small number of extremely large impacts that dominate the treemap visualization.

2

Recorded Landings Spiked in the 1970s and 1980s

The line chart shows a dramatic spike in recorded landings from the 1970s onward. This likely reflects improved detection technology and reporting practices rather than an actual increase in meteorite activity.

3

Deliberate Data Scoping Improves Analysis Quality

Narrowing the dataset from 600+ years to 1900-2000 removed noisy historical records with incomplete coordinates and mass data, resulting in a cleaner, more meaningful analysis.

4

Geographic Concentration Tells a Story

The world map shows clustering across North America, Europe, and parts of Asia, reflecting not just where meteorites fall but where infrastructure exists to detect, recover, and record them.

5

Mass and Frequency Are Not Correlated

The highest-mass years (1920, 1947) do not coincide with the highest-frequency years (1970s-1980s), showing that a single large meteorite can outweigh hundreds of smaller recorded landings.

6

The Treemap Reveals Outlier Years Clearly

The treemap visualization makes it immediately obvious which years dominated total mass. 1920 occupies disproportionate space, making this insight impossible to miss at a glance.
